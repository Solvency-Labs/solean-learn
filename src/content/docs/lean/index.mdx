A **formal proof** is an argument written in so much detail that a *program* can check every step mechanically. A **proof assistant** — we use **Lean 4** — is the program doing the checking. You write definitions and theorems; Lean refuses to call a proof valid unless it's airtight.

That's the mindset shift the rest of T4 is built around. The pages that follow are the path; this page sets the orientation.

## What changes when Lean accepts a proof

The "did the argument really work?" question is *closed*. Lean has type-checked the proof down to its kernel. What's left are two specific risks — the ones that matter:

1. **Did you state the right theorem?** Lean checks proofs, not intent.
2. **What did you assume?** Anything taken as an axiom or hypothesis is *trusted*, not proven.

This is why the project's whole culture is **naming boundaries** — what's proven, what's assumed, what's trusted. "We proved X *under assumption Y*" is the honest unit of work.

## Modeling, then proving

You can't prove things about Solidity bytes directly. The workflow is always:

```
real artifact   ──model──▶   a precise object in Lean   ──prove──▶   a theorem about that object
(ForsVerifier)              (a function / inductive)                ("recover is correct, given keccak")
```

Two distinct risks live here:

- **Modeling gap** — does the Lean object faithfully match the real contract? Tools like [EVMYulLean](/solean-learn/tooling/evmyullean/) shrink this gap by giving Lean precise semantics for the *actual* EVM/Yul.
- **Proof gap** — none, once Lean accepts. That part you get for free.

## Soundness vs. completeness (do not mix these up)

Two words you'll hear constantly:

- **Soundness** — "if Lean (or the verifier) says *valid*, it really is valid." No false accepts. **The security property.**
- **Completeness** — "if it really is valid, it says *valid*." No false rejects.

When in doubt, the scary direction is **soundness** — a soundness bug lets an attacker forge.

## The path through T4

Most of what you need is *reading* skill. You won't be writing many tactics, but you must follow the project's models and theorems. Each step below sets you up to read more of the codebase.

| Step | Page | What you'll be able to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **[Propositions are types](/solean-learn/lean/curry-howard/)** | Explain why a *proof* is just a *term* of a special type — the single idea behind machine-checkable proof. |
| 2 | **[Tactics: building a proof](/solean-learn/lean/tactics/)** | Read a tactic-style proof. Recognize what each common tactic (`intro`, `exact`, `apply`, `rw`, `simp`, `cases`, `induction`) is doing to the goal. |
| 3 | **[Inductive types & induction](/solean-learn/lean/inductive-types-and-proofs/)** | Read the project's data models (statements, results, structures), and follow proofs that case-split on a constructor. This shape *is* most real proofs. |
| 4 | **[A project-shaped proof](/solean-learn/lean/a-project-proof/)** | Follow a SoLean-style "success implies the guards held" theorem from the model, through the statement, through the tactic proof. |
| 5 | **[Lean exercises](/solean-learn/lean/exercises/)** | Test the reading-and-reasoning skills above. |

You don't have to write Lean yourself to be useful on this project. Reading definitions, following theorem statements, and roughly tracking how a proof discharges them is enough.

## Two questions before you start

<Question q="When Lean accepts your proof, which risk is eliminated — and which two remain?">
The **proof gap** is eliminated: the argument is mechanically checked down to the axioms. Two risks remain: (1) **did you state the right theorem?** — Lean checks the proof, not your intent; (2) **what did you assume?** — anything taken as an axiom or hypothesis is trusted, not proven.
</Question>

<Question q="Why does 'we proved X' mean little on its own, without naming the assumptions?">
A theorem is only as strong as its hypotheses. "X" might hold only *under* a strong or even false assumption, in which case the real-world guarantee is weak. The honest unit of work is "we proved X **under assumption Y**." That's why the project insists on naming every boundary and assumption out loud.
</Question>

<Question q="We treat keccak as an assumption. If keccak were later found broken, what happens to our FORS soundness proof?">
The proof still holds *exactly as stated* — it was always "sound **given** keccak behaves like a good hash." What fails is the real-world guarantee, because the *assumption* failed, not the logic. The theorem is conditional; trust lives in the assumption. That's precisely why we [name keccak as a trusted boundary](/solean-learn/task/) rather than pretending it's proven.
</Question>
