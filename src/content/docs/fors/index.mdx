You're comfortable with hash functions and Merkle trees. This track is the bridge to using *just those two ingredients* to sign a message. The first time you see how it works, it's a little surprising — so the rest of T3 walks the path in small steps. This page is the roadmap.

## What you already have

- **Hash function** $H$ — for us, keccak-256. One-way and collision-resistant. [Trusted as a primitive](/solean-learn/task/) in our proof.
- **Merkle tree** — commits to many leaves under a single root; an *authentication path* (the siblings up one level at a time) lets you prove a leaf is in the tree without revealing the others.

Both will appear constantly. You won't need any new math beyond them.

## The big idea (the whole trick in one paragraph)

A hash $H(x)$ is a *commitment* to $x$: publish it before $x$ is known, anyone can later check a claimed $x$ by hashing. Hash-based signatures invert that idea into authentication —

> **Public key = a pile of hashes. Secret key = the preimages of those hashes. Signing = revealing some of those preimages — *and which ones you reveal depends on the message*.**

Verifying = hashing the revealed values and checking they line up with the right public-key entries. Forging without the secrets means inverting $H$, which is exactly what one-wayness rules out.

That single principle drives every scheme in T3. They differ only in:

- **how many secrets** the key holds,
- **how the message picks which to reveal**,
- **how the public key is compressed** (this is where Merkle trees come in — the public key shrinks to a single root),
- **how many signatures** you can safely issue before reuse leaks too much.

<Question q="Why does 'revealing the preimage of a published hash' constitute proof that you knew the secret?">
Because one-wayness of $H$ says: given $h$, you cannot produce an $x$ with $H(x) = h$ — *unless* you knew $x$ to begin with. So a verifier seeing $x$ with $H(x) = h$ has effectively seen a proof that the prover either knew $x$ all along, or broke the hash. Under the standard hash assumption, that's "the prover knew $x$." This is the whole core of hash-based signing.
</Question>

## What's out of scope here

We focus on **how a FORS signature is verified** — that's what the contract does, and what we're proving correct. Real PQ deployments (SPHINCS+/SLH-DSA) also include the *signer* side, key generation, and a giant hypertree above FORS; we touch those only enough to motivate FORS's role.
